NIDS False Positive Rates

Researched by Nicolas Komarov

What Is a False Positive?

A Network Intrusion Detection System (NIDS) monitors all traffic flowing through a network and raises alerts when it detects patterns matching known attack signatures or deviating from normal behavior. A false positive occurs when the system flags completely legitimate, harmless traffic as a threat — a false alarm that wastes analyst time and resources without any actual security incident.

Who Does It Affect?

SOC analysts are hit hardest — reviewing 500–1,000+ alerts per day, most of which are false positives. Small businesses without a dedicated security team often have one IT person responsible for everything. Healthcare and finance face critical consequences when real attacks slip through. Critical infrastructure faces the highest stakes — a missed intrusion can have physical real-world consequences.

92.85%of false IDS cases are false positives in live systems
2,000+false cases tracked over 16 months of real traffic
1,000+alerts per day on an average network
Research published in IEEE Communications Magazine found that across a 16-month real-world study with over 2,000 false cases from live IDS/IPS systems, more than 92.85 percent of all false cases were false positives — primarily because application traffic often does not fully conform to RFC specifications, causing strict detection rules to misidentify legitimate traffic as malicious.— Ho et al., IEEE Commun. Mag., vol. 50, no. 3, 2012 [1]

How Do Hackers Exploit This?

01

Alert Flooding

Attackers generate massive amounts of borderline-suspicious traffic before the real attack, flooding the NIDS so analysts dismiss the genuine malicious packet as more noise.

02

Packet Fragmentation & Evasion

Malicious payloads split across multiple small packets — each looks harmless individually. The target reassembles them and the attack executes; the NIDS never saw a complete threat.

03

Low-and-Slow Attacks

Moving through the network at a crawl — one suspicious action per hour — takes advantage of alert fatigue. Exhausted analysts are far more likely to dismiss it without investigation.

04

Timing Attacks

Real attacks launched during peak false-positive periods — business hours, update windows — when analysts are at maximum alert load and most likely to be overwhelmed.

💡 Fix: Micro-Segmentation + Rule Tuning (Part of Zero Trust)

Dividing the network into small isolated zones means each NIDS sensor monitors a fraction of total traffic, dramatically reducing false positive rates by up to 72%. Combined with regular rule tuning specific to each zone, this is a core pillar of Zero Trust Architecture.

Social Engineering Attacks

Researched by Yao Koumako

What Is Social Engineering?

Social engineering is the practice of manipulating people — rather than systems — into revealing confidential information or taking actions that compromise security. Instead of exploiting a software vulnerability, attackers exploit human psychology: trust, urgency, authority, and fear.

Who Does It Affect?

Large enterprises are targeted through executive impersonation and supply chain attacks. Healthcare workers handle both financial and patient data and are frequently too busy to scrutinize every communication. New employees don't yet know all colleagues and are more likely to comply with requests that seem to come from management. IT helpdesk staff are specifically targeted because they have authority to reset credentials and grant access.

74%of breaches involve the human element
3.4Bphishing emails sent every single day
<60smedian time to click a phishing link
Research establishes that "the human factor remains the weakest link in the security chain, as psychological triggers often override technical safeguards." Attackers exploit cognitive biases — urgency, authority, and reciprocity — that cannot be patched with a software update.— Wang et al., IEEE Access, vol. 9, 2021 [2]

How Attackers Get In

01

Phishing Emails

Deceptive messages impersonating trusted entities designed to steal credentials or redirect users to fake login pages. Modern spear phishing uses personal details from LinkedIn to feel targeted and real.

02

MFA Fatigue Attacks

Attacker already has valid credentials. They trigger repeated MFA push notifications until the victim approves one out of frustration — bypassing what should be a strong security layer.

03

Vishing & Pretexting

Phone calls from attackers posing as IT staff or executives. They construct a believable scenario — "I need your credentials to fix your account before it locks" — and exploit urgency.

04

Exploiting NIDS Alert Fatigue

When security teams are drowning in false positive alerts, behavioral anomalies from a socially engineered employee accessing unusual systems are far more likely to go unnoticed.

💡 Fix: Hardware Security Keys + Zero Trust Identity Verification

Physical FIDO2/WebAuthn hardware keys require the attacker to physically possess the device — MFA fatigue attacks become impossible. Paired with Zero Trust continuous verification, even fully stolen credentials cannot grant access without the physical key and verified device.

⚠️

Hidden Issues Nobody Talks About

The gaps that persist even after basic fixes are in place

These are the blind spots that sophisticated attackers actively exploit — problems that exist beyond false positives and phishing that most organizations never address.
👻

False Negatives

The dangerous flip side — a real attack the NIDS says nothing about. Analysts conditioned to expect false alarms stop scrutinizing the absence of alerts. Attackers who know a NIDS's signature database can craft attacks it simply won't recognize.

🔐

Encrypted Traffic Blindness

The majority of internet traffic is HTTPS/TLS encrypted. A traditional NIDS cannot inspect encrypted packets without a decryption layer. Any attack hidden inside encrypted traffic is effectively invisible.

👤

Insider Threats

NIDS watches for external attackers. A malicious or compromised employee already inside the network, moving laterally with legitimate credentials, generates no suspicious traffic signatures at all.

☁️

Cloud Environment Gaps

Traditional NIDS assumes a physical network with a defined perimeter. As organizations move to AWS, Azure, and hybrid cloud, the perimeter disappears and signature-based detection becomes far less effective.

💸

Compliance Cost Spiral

Organizations with excessive false positive rates fail security audits and must pay for remediation consultants. The financial cost of managing false positives — analyst hours, auto-blocked services, compliance failures — often exceeds the cost of fixing it.

🌐

IPv6 & Protocol Gaps

Many NIDS signature databases are built around IPv4 attack patterns. As IPv6 adoption grows, large gaps emerge. Attackers using IPv6 tunneling or protocol-specific evasion can bypass detection entirely.

💡 Why Zero Trust Addresses These Too

Zero Trust's "assume breach" principle means the system operates as if an attacker is always already inside — making it far more effective against false negatives, insider threats, and encrypted traffic than traditional perimeter-based NIDS. By verifying every access request at the identity and device level, Zero Trust catches behavioral anomalies that signature-based systems miss entirely.

Introduction

For our collaborative technical writing and cybersecurity research project, our team focused on two major cybersecurity issues that continue to affect modern organizations: high false positive rates in Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) and social engineering attacks such as phishing. The purpose of this project was to analyze how these two problems impact organizations, examine how they are interconnected, and develop an educational cybersecurity website presenting Zero Trust Architecture as a unified solution.

The project combined formal research, technical analysis, web development, visual communication, and collaborative presentation design to produce a practical and informative cybersecurity resource that satisfies both the academic goals of the course and delivers real value to anyone learning about network security.

// 02
Research Background

The project began with each team member contributing individual research on a specific cybersecurity problem. Nicolas Komarov researched false positive rates in Network Intrusion Detection Systems, while Yao Koumako focused on phishing and social engineering attacks. After analyzing both papers, the team identified a significant relationship between the two problems that became the foundation of the entire project.

92.85%of false IDS cases in live deployments are false positives [1]

Nicolas's research showed that modern intrusion detection systems generate overwhelming volumes of false alarms, burying real threats in noise and exhausting the analysts responsible for reviewing them. Yao's research showed how attackers exploit human psychology — urgency, authority, and trust — to bypass technical defenses entirely through phishing, MFA fatigue, and impersonation.

// 03
The Core Problem: Two Vulnerabilities, One Gap

Although both issues originate from different areas of information security, they directly feed each other in real-world environments. This connection became the central analytical foundation of the project.

The chain: High false positive rates create alert fatigue — analysts become desensitized and less responsive to genuine warnings. Social engineering attacks take direct advantage of this exhaustion, manipulating users into trusting malicious communications precisely when their vigilance is lowest. Technical vulnerabilities and human vulnerabilities amplify each other.

When a NIDS floods analysts with thousands of meaningless alerts per day, a real attack buried in that noise is far more likely to go uninvestigated. And when an attacker knows that security teams are overwhelmed, a well-crafted phishing email has a significantly higher chance of success.

"The human factor remains the weakest link in the security chain, as psychological triggers often override technical safeguards." — Wang et al., IEEE Access, 2021 [2]

// 05
Proposed Solution: Zero Trust Architecture

Through this research, Zero Trust Architecture emerged as the strongest and most comprehensive solution to both cybersecurity issues. Zero Trust operates on the principle of "never trust, always verify" — no user, device, or connection is automatically trusted regardless of whether it originates from inside or outside the network.

The framework addresses the false positive problem through micro-segmentation — reducing false positive alert volume by up to 72–78% with full implementation. It addresses social engineering through continuous authentication combining hardware security keys, device health checks, and least-privilege access. Even fully compromised credentials cannot grant access without the physical hardware key.

Real-world adoption: Google eliminated VPN entirely through BeyondCorp. Microsoft enforces Zero Trust across 220,000+ employees. The U.S. Federal Government mandated ZTA for all agencies via Executive Order 14028. IBM data shows mature ZTA saves organizations an average of $1.76M per breach.
